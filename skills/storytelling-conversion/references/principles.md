# The eight principles in practice

Use this as the craft reference for substantive work. Each principle has a design decision, a procedure, and an inspection. The exercises can guide self-review; actual reader checks require real readers and must not be invented.

## 1. Predictive processing

Attention depends partly on what the reader expects next. Establish a recognizable pattern, leave a relevant uncertainty unresolved, and deliver an answer with a credible turn. Complete predictability can make an opening easy to ignore; total confusion gives the reader nothing to anticipate.

### Procedure

1. Identify the audience's likely expectation about the situation.
2. Choose a question that matters to that audience. It may concern an outcome, cause, decision, or unexpected detail.
3. Supply enough information for the reader to form a prediction, while withholding the relevant resolution rather than the basic subject.
4. Develop the question and resolve it in a way supported by the setup. A change of interpretation can provide surprise without an exaggerated event.

**Sensory anchor:** Begin with a specific sensation or physical detail that introduces a meaningful question. A repeated click from a machine, a rough edge on a repaired surface, or an unexpected mark on a document can establish a pattern. Use a detail that later matters; unrelated smell, weather, or coffee only adds decoration. When invented, frame the scene as a possibility or illustration.

### Opening inspection

Read the first line alone and ask both:

- Can the audience tell what kind of situation or subject this concerns?
- Is there a reason to keep reading?

Then inspect the payoff: does it answer the real question? Sensationalism, a concealed topic, or a promise that resolves only into "buy this" is a weak substitute for relevant uncertainty.

## 2. The establishing shot

Before asking the audience to follow a problem or accept a solution, establish the world in which it matters. Orient quickly on four dimensions:

| Question | What to establish |
|---|---|
| Where are we? | Setting, task, occasion, or stage in a process |
| Who is here? | The people, roles, or perspective involved |
| What's the vibe? | Emotional atmosphere, relationship, or practical stakes |
| What deserves attention? | The detail, action, or conflict the reader should track |

These need not be four sentences. A specific task can imply a setting, and a carefully chosen interaction can establish mood. Use an analogy when an unfamiliar environment needs to become understandable quickly; map the emotional context and relevant dynamics as well as surface resemblance.

**Opening-only inspection:** Could a reader answer where, who, mood, and focus without reading the explanation that follows? Repair missing orientation. Remove backstory that does not help those answers. Starting in a scene does not require delaying the subject or offer.

## 3. Illustrative words / word pictures

Readers can picture actions and particulars more easily than abstractions such as excellence, efficiency, empowerment, or a comprehensive solution. Convert the central idea into something the audience can imagine. An irrelevant vivid image can dominate memory and displace the intended message.

### Abstraction-to-image pass

1. Mark summary words, broad benefits, and abstract nouns in the draft.
2. Ask what a person could see, hear, touch, or do when that statement is true.
3. Replace the abstraction with the relevant action or object. Develop the detail enough to show its significance.
4. For narrative copy, look for at least one purposeful element of color, pattern, texture, or shine. For example, a row of identical warning marks can communicate a repeated problem. Do not add sensory wording to utility copy solely to meet a quota.
5. Inspect which image is strongest and whether it reinforces the takeaway.

**Close-eyes exercise:** Read the writing aloud, listen to text-to-speech if available, or mentally hear it without looking. What appears in the mind? If the message produces no image, replace summary language. If it produces the wrong image, change emphasis or remove the competing detail. Describe this as self-review unless an actual listener participated.

The practical dual-coding rationale is that imaginable language offers both a verbal expression and an image. Use that as a design aid; do not turn it into a claim that memory, comprehension, or sales necessarily doubles.

## 4. Pick the story structure

Choose the intended emotional experience first, together with the audience's current awareness and readiness. A person who does not recognize the problem needs a different opening from someone comparing a known solution. Then map concrete beats; naming a framework alone accomplishes nothing.

| Desired experience | Structure | Working outline |
|---|---|---|
| Satisfaction, rhythm, anticipation | Save the Cat | Opening image/promise; theme; catalyst/problem; midpoint reframe or solution reveal; low point/stakes; finale/offer; closing image |
| Urgency and momentum | Problem-Agitation-Solution | Recognizable problem; specific credible consequences; response that addresses the mechanism |
| Transformation | Hero's Journey | Familiar world; disruption; attempts and obstacles; changed approach; return with a changed capability or understanding |
| Causal clarity | And-But-Therefore | Relevant situation and continuation; complication; consequence-driven response |

The Save the Cat row is a compressed adaptation of landmarks from a longer 15-beat form, not a claim to reproduce its complete beat sheet. Do not force all of its landmarks into a short post.

For an email launch, spread narrative roles across messages: an opening promise, a catalyst that makes the problem matter, a midpoint that reframes or reveals a response, a credible view of unresolved stakes, then an offer that completes the argument. Each email must also deliver something locally useful. Use legitimate stakes rather than inventing catastrophe or scarcity for the low point.

**Structure inspection:** Can you identify what each beat changes? Does the piece create the intended feeling? Do developments follow causally, or is it a pile of features? Does the ending fulfill the experience promised by the structure?

## 5. Chekhov's gun

Every emphasized detail invites the audience to expect relevance. A conspicuous anecdote, award, number, promise, or object needs a job and often a later payoff. A detail can damage a piece even when it is true: an old award unrelated to the purchase decision can distract from the actual argument.

### Setup/payoff ledger

```text
Emphasized detail or open question:
Expectation it creates:
Where its relevance is established or its promise fulfilled:
Decision: keep / connect / resolve / remove:
```

Track the hook and significant secondary loops. A detail that immediately supplies relevant proof has already done its job; not every fact needs a delayed callback. Close the loops deliberately. A disclosed series may leave a larger question open while resolving its current installment.

### Line-job pass

For every line, identify a meaningful job: build desire, handle an objection, provide proof, create legitimate urgency, orient the reader, advance the causal action, or communicate a material term. Cut a line whose only defense is that it sounds impressive. Keep an effective line instead of rewriting it for novelty. Connect a necessary detail that currently appears random.

**Inspection:** Where does each emphasized setup pay off? What does each line contribute to the audience's decision or experience? Does anything invite attention that the piece never rewards?

## 6. Create the villain: the Mega Mean Mouse

Make the audience's real obstacle specific and recognizable before emphasizing how good the solution is. Vague labels such as disorganization or stress do little explanatory work. A particular process, constraint, habit, or tool limitation can show what the person is actually struggling against.

### Four-step build

1. **Stack consequences.** Follow the immediate problem into its practical and emotional effects. Build a credible connected chain rather than pile on unrelated fears.
2. **Name the external forces.** Identify the circumstances that keep the problem in place: incentives, fragmented information, incompatible processes, competing demands, or unsuitable defaults. Separate observed constraints from proposed interpretations.
3. **List previous attempted solutions.** Use actual audience material when available. If unknown, treat attempts as possibilities to investigate or clearly conditional examples, not a universal history.
4. **Explain why those attempts fail here.** Name the missing mechanism, tradeoff, or mismatch. Show the obstacle's track record and what a response would need to do differently.

Then connect the offer to that mechanism. For a service, explain what changes in the process. For a tool, show how its actual behavior addresses the failure. This is stronger than naming an enemy and immediately switching to a feature list.

**Inspection:** Would the audience recognize its experience in this obstacle? Are the consequences connected? Do external forces explain rather than falsely accuse? Does the proposed solution address why prior attempts were insufficient? Keep real stakes; do not flatten them into a bland inconvenience merely to avoid emotion.

## 7. The sugar-coating principle

Useful information can still be delivered in a forgettable way. Separate the information the audience needs from the experience that helps it stay with that information.

```text
Medicine: the fact, process, formula, distinction, or decision to understand.
Sugar: the audience-valued experience: recognition, suspense, humor,
       conflict, discovery, personality, movement, or participation.
Carrier: the specific scene, demonstration, analogy, or progression.
Recovery: the useful thing the reader can explain or do afterward.
```

Wrap data in a relevant situation, show a process through a decision and its consequences, or explain a dry rule through a conflict it resolves. Humor is one option, not a universal requirement. A quiet discovery can fit a serious or sensitive context better than drama.

**Inspection:** Can you identify both medicine and sugar in the actual draft? Does the carrier make the information easier to follow, or merely interrupt it? If removing all product claims leaves nothing useful and no experience, develop the piece. If removing the drama erases the actual lesson too, make the lesson retrievable.

## 8. Bookending / retrospective coherence

The ending should make the journey purposeful in retrospect. Satisfy the audience's original desire with a resolution that develops, reinterprets, or reverses an opening element. A repeated phrase without changed meaning is a decorative callback.

### Procedure

1. Specify the emotional starting point and intended ending point.
2. Plant an image that can change significance, a question that can be answered differently, or a belief the piece will challenge.
3. Develop the events or explanation that make that change credible.
4. Return to the planted element with the new understanding. An earned turn can satisfy the expected desire through a less predictable route.
5. Connect the CTA to the resolved understanding instead of appending an unrelated sales command.

**Removal inspection:** Remove the closing return mentally. Does the piece lose necessary resolution, transformation, or meaning? If it remains complete in the same way, the callback may be ornamental. Strengthen the resolution or omit the ornament. A direct factual answer only needs to resolve the stated question.

## Using the method responsibly

These are craft decisions and exercises. Do not present simplified brain explanations or unsupported numerical improvements as proof of effectiveness. Keep accurate facts, credible stakes, and clear fictional framing while allowing imagery, conflict, humor, surprise, and emotional movement to do their work.
